Spare.

You know how to budget. You pay your bills. But somehow you’re still not saving as much as you’d like. Spare tracks the money left after income, bills and a savings goal — and where it actually goes.

Sector 1 — why

Visibility, not a lecture.

The user Spare is for already understands budgeting. They pay their bills on time and aren’t in crisis — they simply can’t account for where the leftover money goes. They don’t want to be taught; they want to see the money that is already theirs to spend.

The defining moment is standing at a counter, phone in hand, logging what you just bought. Everything else — reviewing the month, adjusting the goal — happens afterwards and less often.

Sector 2 — how

Manual entry is the feature.

Spare has no bank connection and never will. Typing the purchase in is the act that creates awareness; automatic import would remove the only moment where you actually confront the spend. And every purchase records two more things — was it a want or a need, and how did it feel — because pattern detection over feelings is the reason the app exists.

Setup once: income after tax, bills (a single figure or itemised), savings goal. The discretionary budget is derived: income − bills − savings goal. Daily: add a purchase — amount, category, want/need, a note, the date. Periodically: dashboard, category breakdown, trends, calendar and ledger for the month; recurring charges for the things that come back.

Next.js App Router with Supabase for auth and Postgres (row-level security per user), shadcn/ui on Tailwind, Recharts for the breakdowns, and Stripe for a free tier and a $1.99/month Pro. It ships a web manifest so it lives on an iPhone home screen — mobile-first, desktop as the fallback, entry speed on a phone ahead of desktop density.

Sector 3 — what stuck

Lessons.

  • Removing a feature can be the product: no bank sync is what makes the logging moment happen.
  • Purchase dates are plain YYYY-MM-DD strings, never a Date — timezones have no business in “what did I buy on Tuesday”.
